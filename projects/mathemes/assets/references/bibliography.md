# Mathemes — Annotated Bibliography

## Primary Sources (Anti-Eponymy)

### Henwood & Rival (1980)
**"Eponymy in Mathematical Nomenclature: What's in a Name, and What Should Be?"**
*The Mathematical Intelligencer* 2(4), 204–205.
- **URL**: https://ivanrival.com/docs/eponymy.pdf
- **Contribution**: First published manifesto against mathematical eponymy. Invokes Darwin's "naming vs. describing" distinction. Proposes mining the vernacular for descriptive terms.
- **What it lacks**: No system. Just principles: (1) use descriptive names where they exist, (2) don't name trivial things, (3) use everyday language. No compositional grammar, no generative mechanism.

### Beretta (2019)
**"Names as Rewards: The Ambiguous Role of Eponyms in the History of Science"**
*Nuncius* 34(2), 219–235. DOI: 10.1163/18253911-03402002
- **URL**: https://brill.com/view/journals/nun/34/2/article-p219_2.xml
- **Contribution**: Frames scientific eponymy as "one of the most effective reward systems in Western science." Traces genealogy from Hellenistic antiquity through Renaissance proliferation.
- **What it lacks**: Historical analysis only. No reform proposal. No constructive alternative.

### White (2019–ongoing)
**"Better Terms for Mathematical Concepts"**
Personal website / ongoing catalog.
- **URL**: https://www.zipcon.net/~swhite/docs/math/better_terms.html
- **Contribution**: The most exhaustive ad hoc rename catalog extant. Hundreds of proposals across geometry, calculus, linear algebra, analysis, topology, algebra. Includes etymological analysis and comparative naming across languages.
- **What it lacks**: No underlying system. No generative grammar. Each rename is an independent proposal.

### Nautilus (2020) [now 404]
**"Why Mathematicians Should Stop Naming Things After Each Other"**
*Nautilus*, Issue 89.
- **URL**: http://m.nautil.us/issue/89/the-dark-side/why-mathematicians-should-stop-naming-things-after-each-other (dead link)
- **Contribution**: Popular-audience case against eponyms. Thurston's complaint about "Thurston's theorem about Thurston maps."
- **What it lacks**: No constructive alternative. Jim Bagrow's commentary actually argues the opposite of our thesis — that abstractness makes descriptive naming harder (we prove it forces it).

### Bagrow (2020)
**Commentary on Nautilus article**
- **URL**: https://bagrow.com/blog/2020/09/15/why-mathematicians-should-stop-naming-things-after-each-other/
- **Contribution**: States "When the results are 'pure' you often have no intuition upon which to anchor your name." This is the claim our category theory paradox directly refutes.

## Primary Sources (Naming Systems in Other Sciences)

### IUPAC Nomenclature
International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry, nomenclature standards (1919–present).
- **URL**: https://iupac.org/what-we-do/nomenclature/
- **Key insight**: Compositional morpheme-based naming that mirrors molecular structure. The name IS the structure. Proves generative naming works at scale.

### Linnaean Taxonomy
Carl Linnaeus, *Systema Naturae* (1735).
- **Key insight**: Hierarchical binomial nomenclature. Name encodes phylogenetic position. Succeeded because it mapped the compositional structure of biological classification.

### PhyloCode
International Society for Phylogenetic Nomenclature (2000s).
- **Key insight**: Reform attempt on Linnaean taxonomy. Mixed results. Demonstrates difficulty of changing entrenched nomenclature. Key lesson: dual naming (systematic + traditional) is the practical adoption path.

### ISO 80000-2:2019
**Quantities and Units — Part 2: Mathematics**
- **Contribution**: Standardizes mathematical symbols and notation. Does NOT address concept naming.

### Mathematics Subject Classification (MSC)
American Mathematical Society / zbMATH.
- **Contribution**: Numerical taxonomy for mathematical areas. Categorizes, but does not name.

## Eponymy Meta-Literature

### Stigler's Law of Eponymy
Stigler, S. (1980). "Stigler's Law of Eponymy." *Transactions of the New York Academy of Sciences* 39, 147–157.
- Also: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stigler's_law_of_eponymy
- **Contribution**: "No scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer." The law itself exemplifies its own claim.

### Brown's Law of Mathematical Eponymy (2022)
*Math Horizons* 30(2), November 2022.
- **URL**: https://digitaleditions.sheridan.com/article/Brown%E2%80%99s+Law+of+Mathematical+Eponymy/4372702/766255/article.html

### Zeilberger Opinion 158 (2017)
**Satirical**: "The AMS Should Not Force People to Stop Using Historical Names... and Replace Them by AMS-ID-Numbers"
- **URL**: https://sites.math.rutgers.edu/~zeilberg/Opinion158.html
- **Contribution**: April Fool's satire. The joke works because no real alternative system exists — the only thing he could imagine replacing eponyms with was bureaucratic numbering.

## Cognitive Science Foundations (to research)

### Sweller — Cognitive Load Theory
Sweller, J. (1988). "Cognitive Load During Problem Solving." *Cognitive Science* 12(2), 257–285.
- **Relevance**: Opaque names impose extraneous cognitive load. Descriptive names convert it to germane cognitive load.

### Paivio — Dual Coding Theory
Paivio, A. (1986). *Mental Representations: A Dual Coding Approach*. Oxford University Press.
- **Relevance**: Names that evoke spatial/sensory imagery are better retained. Category theory's spatial vocabulary (pullback, lifting, fiber) triggers dual coding.

### Miller — Chunking
Miller, G.A. (1956). "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two." *Psychological Review* 63(2), 81–97.
- **Relevance**: Working memory holds ~4 chunks. Descriptive multi-word names are more chunks, but each chunk carries meaning.

## Linguistic Foundations (to research)

### ISO 704
**Terminology Work — Principles and Methods**
- **Relevance**: International standard for term formation in specialized domains. Defines semantic transparency and other key principles.

### Ranganathan — Faceted Classification
Ranganathan, S.R. (1933). *Colon Classification*. Madras Library Association.
- **Relevance**: Every concept describable along multiple independent facets. Mathematical objects have structure, property, operation, domain, and scope facets.

## Philosophy of Language (to research)

### Kripke — Naming and Necessity
Kripke, S. (1980). *Naming and Necessity*. Harvard University Press.
- **Relevance**: Eponymous names as rigid designators (point but don't describe) vs. descriptive names as definite descriptions (carry verification conditions).

## Category Theory Naming Origins

### Eilenberg & Mac Lane (1945)
**"General Theory of Natural Equivalences"**
*Transactions of the AMS* 58(2), 231–294.
- **Contribution**: Founded category theory. Mac Lane later noted they were "purloining words from the philosophers" — Category from Aristotle/Kant, Functor from Carnap, "natural transformation" from informal parlance.
- **Key insight**: The naming was a DESIGN choice. They built vocabulary as a primary artifact, not an afterthought.
