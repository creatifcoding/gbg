## Key Findings So Far

### 1. BHS Architecture (from Source #1)
Four sorting technology tiers, each with different installation complexity:
- **Traditional conveyors** — fixed layouts, mechanical diverters. Simplest install. Small/medium airports.
- **Crossbelt sorters (CBS)** — individual carriers with belts. Gentler handling. Modular. JCK likely installs these.
- **Tilt-tray** — trays on continuous loop, tilt to discharge. Older tech, being replaced by CBS.
- **DCV/ICS** — independent vehicles on track networks. Highest complexity. Major hubs (Heathrow T5 — where JCK worked).

### 2. Installation Scope (from Source #6 — Charlotte Douglas Airport)
Real JCK-comparable project scope:
- Outbound: ticket counter, oversize, curbside, FIS re-check conveyor lines
- Inbound: incline plate claim devices, roller-bed conveyors, header steel, hanger supports, catwalks
- **All night shift** (airport operates during day)
- **JIT delivery** — no staging space inside airport
- Warehousing + logistics management included in scope

### 3. Installation Phases (from Source #5 — Beck & Pollitzer)
1. **Planning/Prep** — site evaluation, design collaboration, utility verification
2. **Project Management** — timelines, resource allocation, risk management, dedicated PM
3. **Safety** — emergency stops, guard rails, sensors, regulatory compliance
4. **Installation** — mechanical (frames, belts, motors, rollers, mezzanines) THEN electrical (control panels, sensors, wiring, control system config)
5. **Testing/Commissioning** — dry runs, load tests, gradual load introduction, personnel training

### 4. The Integrator-Contractor Relationship
- **Integrator** (Vanderlande, Beumer, etc.) = designs and supplies the system. Owns the technology.
- **Installation contractor** (JCK) = physically installs what the integrator designed. Provides labor, supervision, logistics.
- Integrator sets specs, contractor executes. Contractor must comply with integrator's design/production guidelines.
- Contractor often works under integrator as subcontractor, not directly for the airport.

### 6. Commissioning Sequence (from Source #46 — Advanced Intralogistics)

Four distinct phases, each a quality gate:

1. **Site Readiness** — equipment installed to agreed level of completeness. Controls team can begin.
2. **Static Testing** — I/O checkout. Programs loaded into PLC. Network connectivity established. Verify all inputs/outputs wired correctly from device to I/O card. Done by functional area.
3. **Dynamic Testing** — Logic enabled, control established. Verify conveyors and field devices function as designed (flow control, jams, full conditions). Start with no product → single products → small volume → methodically increase.
4. **Customer Acceptance Testing** — Customer signs off that system meets contract specs. Generates punch list.
5. **Punch List** — document minor anomalies, errors, issues. Assigned to functional leaders for correction by deadline.

### 7. I/O Checkout Details (from Source #20 — Allied Circuits)

Required documents for I/O checkout:
1. **I/O List** — most important. Table of all input/output points. Device tags, descriptions, signal types, scaling info.
2. **Schematic Wiring Diagram (SWD)** — graphical representation of control panel wiring. Used for visual verification of signal paths and tracking progress.
3. **P&ID** — Process and Instrumentation Diagram. Shows interconnectedness of components. Used to find physical location of equipment.
4. **Network & System Configuration** — Ethernet, serial, Modbus config for communication devices.

### 8. EVM Formulas (from Source #33 — Gather Insights)

Core measurements:
- **PV** (Planned Value) = budgeted cost of work scheduled
- **EV** (Earned Value) = BAC × % Complete (physical achievement)
- **AC** (Actual Cost) = actual spend
- **BAC** (Budget at Completion) = total approved budget

Seven essential formulas:
- CV = EV - AC (cost variance)
- SV = EV - PV (schedule variance)
- CPI = EV / AC (cost performance index; >1.0 = under budget)
- SPI = EV / PV (schedule performance index; >1.0 = ahead)
- EAC = BAC / CPI (estimate at completion)
- ETC = EAC - AC (estimate to complete)
- TCPI = (BAC - EV) / (BAC - AC) (required efficiency to hit budget)

Critical rule: CPI rarely recovers more than 10% once it drops below threshold at 20% completion mark.

S-curves: plot PV, EV, AC over time. Gap between PV and EV = schedule variance. Gap between EV and AC = cost variance. Need 3+ periods before useful.

### 10. BHS Commissioning & ORAT Framework (from Source #29 — LinkedIn Practitioner's Guide)

**"A BHS doesn't fail because of conveyors. It fails because of integration."**

Modern BHS commissioning is strategic, not just technical:
- **Controls-first thinking** — HLC/PLC/SCADA stack determines real performance
- **Validate carrier dispatch logic early** — before mechanical is even complete
- **Stress-test merge points and recirculation paths**
- **Test alarm prioritization and fallback states**
- **Data interoperability** with AODB (Airport Operational Database), BRS (Baggage Reconciliation System), DCS (Departure Control System)

**Throughput testing must simulate real conditions:**
- Uneven flight banks (not steady-state)
- Airline-specific baggage profiles
- Last-minute check-ins / rush bags
- Seasonal load variance
- Burst-load, imbalanced load, high recirculation, failover cycles

**ORAT (Operational Readiness and Airport Transfer) requires:**
- Cross-vendor collaboration: security screening, airlines, ground handlers, airport IT, fire/MEP
- Joint tabletop rehearsals and full flows before go-live
- Performance metrics: delivered BPH, MTBF, MTTR, recovery time, mis-route rate, choke-point frequency

**Knowledge transfer at handover:**
- Shift-level SOPs
- Fault-response playbooks
- Spare-parts strategies
- Hands-on maintenance drills
- SCADA/BHS dashboard training
- Airline-specific baggage workflows

**Key acronyms for the domain:**
- ORAT = Operational Readiness and Airport Transfer
- BPH = Bags Per Hour
- MTBF = Mean Time Between Failures
- MTTR = Mean Time To Repair
- AODB = Airport Operational Database
- BRS = Baggage Reconciliation System
- DCS = Departure Control System
- HLC = High-Level Control (system-wide routing logic)
- PLC = Programmable Logic Controller (device-level control)
- SCADA = Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition

### 11. 7-Step Conveyor Installation Process (from Source — Lafayette Engineering)

1. **Planning & Design** — Assess facility layout, material flow, throughput needs. Create engineering drawings. Obtain approvals.
2. **Preparation** — Clear site, secure permits, ensure safety compliance.
3. **Installation** — Foundation/support structures → component assembly → alignment verification.
4. **Testing** — Initial no-load tests → adjustments for optimization.
5. **Commissioning** — Full-load comprehensive testing → staff training.
6. **Maintenance** — Regular inspections (monthly) + preventive maintenance + annual comprehensive checks.
7. **Optimization** — Continuous improvement cycle.

Timeline: Several weeks to a few months depending on complexity/size.

### 13. FAT/SAT/FIT Protocol Deep Dive (from Source #25 — Sarom Global)

**FAT (Factory Acceptance Testing):**
- Conducted at manufacturer's facility BEFORE shipment
- Verifies design compliance
- Different checklists per equipment type:
  - Mechanical: pressure testing, material certification, dimensional verification
  - Control panels: wiring verification, interlock logic, I/O point checks
  - SCADA/DCS/PLC: functional testing, HMI config, data comms, cybersecurity
  - Instrumentation: calibration certificates, loop checks, transmitter verification

**FIT (Factory Integration Test) — NEW in IEC 62381:2024:**
- Multi-vendor systems tested TOGETHER at factory before shipment
- Catches interface/integration mismatches BEFORE site arrival
- "Skipping FIT may appear to save money initially, but it increases the likelihood of costly rework"

**SAT (Site Acceptance Testing):**
- At client site AFTER installation
- Checks real-world conditions: live utilities, interconnected systems, site-specific factors
- Core checks: visual inspection, PLC/DCS I/O verification, control systems/interlocks, safety devices/alarms, operator training readiness

**3-Phase FAT Protocol (IEC 62381:2024):**
1. Planning — scope from customer specs, standards, drawings
2. Documentation & Engineering — ITP, P&IDs, GA drawings, calibration certs
3. Testing Execution — full functional testing, punch list generation

**Key standards:**
- IEC 62381:2024 — governs FAT, FIT, SAT, SIT for automation systems
- ISA 18.2 / IEC 62682 — alarm management
- IEC 62443-3-3 — cybersecurity for control systems

**Cost insight:** "Fixing a defect during implementation costs 4-5× more than fixing it during design" (IBM Systems Sciences Institute)

### 16. Common Conveyor Installation Defects (from Sources — Ultraplast, Alltracon)

**Top 7 installation errors that cause rework/NCRs:**
1. **Misalignment** — cascading issues: product jamming, chain wear, tracking problems, energy waste. Requires laser alignment tools.
2. **Incorrect belt/chain tensioning** — over-tension destroys sprockets/bearings/drives; under-tension causes slippage. Must follow OEM specs.
3. **Poor lubrication** — wrong grade, missed points, or over-application attracting contaminants.
4. **Thermal expansion ignored** — warping, jamming in temperature-variable environments (warehouses, airports).
5. **Component incompatibility** — generic fasteners with premium components cause galvanic corrosion.
6. **Inadequate load testing** — approved on no-load, fails under production conditions.
7. **Rushed commissioning** — compressed schedules → abbreviated testing → ongoing problems.

**Additional installation mistakes (Alltracon):**
- Skipping site survey (spatial constraints, structural support, obstructions)
- Not involving key stakeholders early (ops, maintenance, IT)
- Ignoring environmental factors (dust, humidity, chemicals, temperature)
- Wrong conveyor type for the product
- Underestimating peak load capacity
- Skipping controls/automation integration
- Poor electrical/motor wiring (grounding, surge protection, load distribution)
- No preventive maintenance plan

### 18. Conveyor Safety / OSHA Requirements (from Source — BisonConvey)

**Three OSHA frameworks for conveyor operations:**
1. **LOTO (29 CFR 1910.147)** — Lockout/Tagout. Must address: electrical, hydraulic/pneumatic, gravity, and stored mechanical energy from belt tension.
2. **Machine guarding (29 CFR 1910.219 / 1910.212)** — Guards on head/tail pulleys, take-ups, drives, nip points. Must prevent reach-under/over/through.
3. **Construction conveyors (29 CFR 1926.555)** — Specific conveyor requirements for construction sites.

**ASME B20.1-2024 updates:**
- Remote E-stops must identify which equipment they control
- Strengthened maintenance/lubrication expectations
- New dust hazard section

**OSHA penalty levels (Jan 2025):**
- Serious violation: $16,550 per violation
- Willful/repeated: $165,514 per violation
- LOTO and machine guarding are among most-cited standards every year

**Practical safety concerns during AMH installation:**
- Working at height (catwalks, mezzanines, overhead conveyors)
- LOTO during belt changes, jam clearing, tracking adjustments
- Nip points at head/tail pulleys and take-up units
- Falling material from elevated conveyor runs
- Confined space work in tunnels (JCK did the T3-T5 tunnel at Heathrow)
- Airport-specific: security clearances, restricted access, active operations nearby
- Night shift work (Charlotte Douglas example — all work done at night)

1. Mistracking (belt misalignment) — frame, roller, debris, off-center loading
2. Belt slippage — tension, worn pulleys, overweight, cold temps
3. Seized rollers — dirt, no lube, misalignment
4. Blockages — accumulation, sharp edges, irregular shapes
5. Material carryback — sticky materials, ineffective cleaners
6. Material spillage — overloading, misalignment, gaps at transfer points
7. Belt tears and seam rips — sharp objects, bad splicing, excessive tension
8. Excessive noise — worn components, no lube, misalignment
9. Overheating motors — overloading, poor ventilation, worn components
10. Chain wear — no lube, improper tension, debris
11. Bearing failures — contaminated lube, misalignment, overload
12. **Control system malfunctions** — faulty wiring, software glitches, sensor failures
13. Belt misalignment from frame issues — warped frame, weak supports, vibration
14. **Speed fluctuations** — faulty VFD, motor issues, load inconsistencies
15. **Unexpected shutdowns** — electrical faults, safety sensor false triggers, mechanical jams

**SIOS opportunity:** Items 12, 14, 15 are diagnostics that SIOS's AI knowledge base + alarm system can help field crews resolve faster.

### 27. Commissioning Best Practices (from AMD Machines — 2,500+ custom systems delivered)

**Production ramp-up phases (industry standard):**
1. **Dry cycling** — no product. Verify motion sequences, timing, handshake logic. Watch for collisions, missed sensors, race conditions.
2. **Low-rate production** — reduced speed with real product. Measure critical dimensions. Check Cpk. Run destructive test samples for welds.
3. **Rate verification** — target speed, 2-4 hours continuous. Track OEE: availability, performance, quality.
4. **Endurance run** — 24-hour or multi-shift at full rate. Surfaces: thermal drift, consumable depletion, debris accumulation, operator fatigue.

**I/O checkout detail:**
- Physically actuate each sensor → confirm correct PLC bit toggles
- Force each output individually → confirm correct device energizes
- Analog signals: verify 4-20mA at zero, mid-range, and full scale
- "A pressure transducer that reads 3.8 mA at zero will cause control problems that are difficult to diagnose later"

**Common commissioning mistakes (from 2,500+ projects):**
1. **Compressing schedule** — "the single most common and most costly mistake"
2. **Skipping FAT** — "discovering problems after installation costs far more"
3. **Insufficient operator involvement** — "operators who first see the system on production day will struggle"
4. **Incomplete punch lists** — "verbal agreements to fix things later get forgotten"

### 29. CPI/SPI Worked Examples (from EisnerAmper)

**Concrete worked example ($450K project):**

SPI < 1 (behind schedule):
- Budget: $450K. EV (40% done): $180K. PV (50% planned): $225K. AC: $225K
- SPI = $180K / $225K = **0.8** → behind schedule
- CPI = $180K / $225K = **0.8** → over budget

SPI > 1 (ahead of schedule):
- Same budget. EV (40% done): $180K. PV (20% planned): $90K. AC: $160K
- SPI = $180K / $90K = **2.0** → ahead of schedule
- CPI = $180K / $160K = **1.125** → within budget

**Key insight for SIOS:** "The CPI and SPI don't pinpoint specific areas for improvement." → SIOS needs to break EVM down by installation zone/trade, not just project-level. A project CPI of 0.95 can mask Zone A at 1.10 and Zone C at 0.78.

### 30. BHS Project Delivery Methods (from BEUMER)

**Two contracting models JCK operates within:**

1. **Design-Bid-Build** (traditional):
   - Airport prescribes specific design
   - Contractors bid to build the prescribed design
   - Lowest bid wins → locked into that design
   - Risk: change orders, claims, delays, budget overruns
   - JCK's role: subcontractor to the winning bidder

2. **Design-Build** (modern, preferred):
   - Contractor/supplier responsible for BOTH design and build
   - Collaborative, customized designs
   - Better utilization of vendor-specific equipment
   - Smaller footprint, lower lifecycle cost
   - JCK's role: subcontractor to the design-build integrator (Vanderlande/BEUMER)

**Critical context for SIOS:** JCK needs to track progress against BOTH delivery models. Design-build projects may have evolving scope (design changes during construction), requiring flexible task/work order management. Design-bid-build projects have fixed scope but tight budgets.

### 32. Conveyor Installation Cost Factors (from Horizon Industrial)

**Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) framework:**
1. **Initial costs** — system type, components (belts, valve gates, controls), quality, capacity
2. **Labor costs** — calculated hourly, varies by complexity, location, contractor experience

### 33. Crew Sizing Formula (from MEP Academy)

**The fundamental crew sizing equation for construction:**

```
Crew Size = (Total Quantity / Productivity Rate) / Available Days
```

**Example:**
- 3,000 feet of duct to install
- Productivity: 50 feet/day/worker
- Schedule: 10 days
- Crew needed: 3,000 / 50 = 60 worker-days / 10 = **6 workers**

**Crew composition (union construction):**
- General Foreman
- Foreman
- Journeymen (skilled trade workers)
- Apprentices (various experience levels)
- Ratio of journeymen to apprentices dictated by local union rules

**Productivity rate sources:**
- Historical data from cost codes on completed projects
- Units: LF/Day (linear feet/day), Hours/Each, Lbs/Hr
- **Cost codes** track time per task type — this is EXACTLY what SIOS should capture

**SIOS connection:** SIOS needs to:
1. Track actual hours per task type per worker (building the productivity database)
2. Compare against historical rates (earned value)
3. Calculate crew sizing for future lookahead planning
4. Handle union labor ratio requirements in crew planning

**Factors affecting crew size:**
- Project schedule (duration constraints)
- Scope per scheduled duration per area
- Productivity rate per item (from historical data)
- Weather / extreme conditions
- Union ratio requirements (journeymen:apprentice ratios)
- Available working area (airport constraints — limited staging)
