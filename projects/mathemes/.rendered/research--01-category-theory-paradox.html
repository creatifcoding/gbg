The Category Theory Paradox

Status: Draft — original argument, no known prior art.

Thesis

Category theory — the most abstract branch of mathematics, the one most divorced from physical intuition — produced the most semantically transparent vocabulary in all of mathematics. This is not coincidence but causation: abstraction forced descriptive naming because no physical intuition existed to subsidize opaque terminology.

This paradox has a corollary that indicts the rest of mathematics: if the most abstract field proved that descriptive naming scales to arbitrary abstraction, then every field that defaults to eponymous naming is leaving pedagogical value on the table — not because descriptive naming can't handle their subject, but because they never needed it badly enough to bother.

The Argument

Premise 1: Category theory's vocabulary is overwhelmingly descriptive

The core vocabulary is spatial, mechanical, and metaphorical — despite the subject having no space, no mechanics, no physical referent:

Term Semantic Content Cognitive Trigger
Pullback Spatial — "pulling back" along a map Tug, retraction
Pushforward Spatial — "pushing forward" through a map Force, projection
Lifting Spatial — raising from base to total space Elevation, gravity
Fiber Structural — the pre-image thread over a point Textile, strand
Section Spatial — a cut through a bundle Cross-section
Cone / Cocone Geometric — a vertex with arrows to a diagram Shape
Limit / Colimit Convergence — the universal approximation Approaching
Natural Intrinsic — no arbitrary choices involved Unforced
Adjoint Relational — paired in a universal way Alongside
Forgetful Cognitive — discards structure Memory loss
Universal Scope — unique up to isomorphism Canonical
Representable Epistemic — knowable through a single object Observable

Premise 2: This is anomalous relative to the rest of mathematics

Fields with strong physical grounding — waves, heat, mechanics, probability — name their core concepts after people:

Field Physical Intuition Naming Style
Fourier analysis Vibrating strings, sound Eponymous
Probability theory Dice, coins, measurement Eponymous
Differential geometry Surfaces, curvature Eponymous
Linear algebra Spatial transformations Eponymous
Category theory None Descriptive

Premise 3: The causal mechanism is intuition subsidy

In physically grounded fields, the concept's meaning is carried by the physics. You can feel a vibrating string. You can see heat diffusing. The physical intuition does the explanatory work that the name should do. This means the name is free — it doesn't need to carry semantic weight, so it can carry credit instead.

Category theory has no such subsidy. There is no vibrating string. There is no physical phenomenon grounding "the universal property of the product in a locally small category." The name is the only intuition-delivery mechanism available. If the names were opaque, the concepts would be unthinkable.

Therefore: abstraction imposed selective pressure on naming quality. Fields with physical intuition relaxed that pressure, allowing names to degrade into credit stamps.

Premise 4: This proves descriptive naming scales to any abstraction level

If descriptive naming works for the most abstract structures in mathematics — structures where even sets are too concrete — then the argument "abstract concepts can't be named descriptively" is empirically falsified.

Bagrow (2020) writes: "When the results are 'pure' you often have no intuition upon which to anchor your name." Category theory refutes this directly. The intuition doesn't come from the domain — it comes from the name itself, via spatial and mechanical metaphor.

Conclusion

The fields closest to physicality named things after people because the physical intuition was already there. The field furthest from physicality named things descriptively because it had to.

This is not tradition. It is negligence preserved by the subsidy of physical intuition.

Implications for Mathemes

The paradox is not just a philosophical observation — it is the existence proof for the Mathemes system. Category theory demonstrates that:

  1. Descriptive naming is feasible at arbitrary levels of abstraction
  2. Spatial and mechanical metaphor can carry meaning even in non-physical domains
  3. Compositional naming (where the name encodes defining properties) aids comprehension
  4. Good naming is a design choice, not a discovery — Eilenberg and Mac Lane built their vocabulary deliberately

Prior Art Search (May 2025)

Extensive search found no published formulation of this specific argument:

  • Henwood & Rival (1980): Advocate descriptive names but don't contrast with category theory
  • Beretta (2019): Frames eponyms as reward system but makes no field comparisons
  • White (2019+): Catalogs renames without the causal argument
  • Bagrow (2020): Actually argues the opposite — that abstractness makes naming harder
  • MathOverflow, nLab, HSM StackExchange: Note category theory's naming is descriptive, but don't frame it as paradoxical or draw the causal inference
  • Lakoff & Núñez (2000): Discuss spatial metaphor in math but not naming reform
  • Philosophy of mathematical practice literature: Discusses notation, not naming

The individual observations are scattered. The synthesis — that abstraction forced good naming, which retroactively proves the physical fields had no excuse — appears to be novel.
